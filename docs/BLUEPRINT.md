## Guardrails to settle before launch

Three features in this plan carry real legal exposure: telling women to buy a specific stock, earning affiliate money from brokers, and a tool that names the "best investment" for a person's situation. Each can be kept, if it is shaped carefully. Have a securities attorney review the app before launch; this section is a checklist for that meeting, not legal advice.

**1. Teach Anthropic as a case study, not a buy call.** Saying "get on board and buy Anthropic" while also earning broker referral fees can look like a paid stock recommendation. Frame it as: "Here is how to evaluate a mega-IPO, using Anthropic as the live example. Here is what would make it a fit for you, and what would not." Include the risks plainly: the S-1 shows a large net loss, valuation talk has already moved from about $2T to about $1.5T, and most everyday investors buy at the first trade, often above the IPO price.

**2. Education, not personalized advice.** Calculators and archetypes should show ranges and general principles ("women in this situation often prioritize an emergency fund and low-cost index funds"). Avoid outputs like "You should put $X into Y." Personalized recommendations for compensation can require registration as an investment adviser.

**3. Disclose every affiliate relationship.** The FTC requires a clear disclosure next to the link, not only in a footer. Use a line such as: "Dr. Cherie Banks may earn a commission if you open an account through this link. This does not change our educational content." Some broker programs also restrict how you can describe them; read each program's terms.

**4. Consent for texts and emails.** Phone numbers for SMS marketing need express written consent (TCPA): an unchecked box, separate from email consent, with "Msg & data rates may apply. Reply STOP to opt out." Email needs an unsubscribe link and a physical mailing address (CAN-SPAM).

**5. Protect what you collect.** A privacy policy, encrypted storage, and no sale of data. Never collect Social Security numbers, account numbers, or passwords, and never connect to members' banks. Calculator inputs and the optional Wealth Journey totals stay private to each member (see "Privacy and security").

**6. Note the AI conflict.** This blueprint was drafted with Claude, which is made by Anthropic. Say so in the Anthropic module so readers can weigh it.

**7. Tone on wealth.** Present the Apple comparison honestly: many celebrated tech IPOs (for example, Facebook in 2012) traded below their offer price for months, and some never recovered. Faith-based framing is strongest when it pairs boldness with wisdom (Luke 14:28, count the cost).

## App overview and user journey

Her Kingdom Wealth is a mobile-first web app (installable on phones) that captures each woman's details, places her in a tier, and walks her through modules, tools, and resources until she reaches Kingdom Builder.

**User journey**

1. **Landing page** — Hero with Dr. Cherie Banks, mission line, "Start Free" button, free guide download offer.
2. **Sign-up** — First name, last name, email, and mobile number (all required except last name); an optional invitation code with clear instructions and an instant confirmation ("My Sister's Keeper: 12 months of complimentary Premium will be unlocked with your account"); separate email and SMS consent boxes; the safety question (see "Safety question and Safety Exit"); the educational-content acknowledgment; a "How we protect your information" panel; password (or magic link).
3. **Welcome and devotional** — Short video or note from Dr. Banks; Matthew 25 framing.
4. **Placement quiz** — 10 questions on knowledge and experience; result = Seed, Sower, Steward, or Kingdom Builder. She can browse any tier.
5. **Archetype profile** — Life-stage and risk questions (see Investor archetypes); result = her archetype card and risk band.
6. **Dashboard** — Tier progress ring, next lesson, Anthropic IPO spotlight, calculators, "Verse of the week."
7. **Modules** — Short lessons (5–10 min), each with a key idea, a scripture, a checklist, and a 3-question quiz.
8. **Level up** — Passing a tier's quizzes and checklist unlocks a gold badge and the next tier (with a celebration screen).
9. **Ongoing** — Weekly email and text nudges, new market lessons, invitations to Dr. Banks' mentoring or live sessions.

**Main screens:** Home, Learn (tiers and modules), Tools (calculators), Anthropic IPO Case Study, Wealth Vehicles, Platforms, Legends, Resources, Profile, Admin (for you only).

## The four tiers and module curriculum

Each tier has 8–10 short modules, a scripture theme, a practical checklist, and clear criteria to level up. Every module ends with "Apply it": one action she takes that week.

### Tier 1 — Seed (Novice)

Goal: build the foundation and make her first investment safely. Theme: Luke 16:10, faithful in little.

1. Money mindset and stewardship: what God says about wealth
2. Your money map: net worth, budget, the 50/30/20 starting point
3. Emergency fund first: 3–6 months of expenses in a high-yield savings account
4. Good debt vs bad debt; paying off high-interest cards before investing
5. What a stock, bond, ETF, and index fund are (plain language)
6. What an IPO is, and how everyday investors actually get shares
7. What a broker is, how to choose one, and how to open an account step by step
8. Your first buy: fractional shares, a market vs limit order, a broad index fund
9. Compound growth: the eighth wonder (calculator walkthrough)
10. Fraud red flags: guaranteed returns, pressure, "secret" stocks

Level up: emergency fund started, account opened, first index fund purchase, quizzes passed.

### Tier 2 — Sower (Some knowledge)

Goal: invest with a plan, not on impulse. Theme: Proverbs 21:5, the plans of the diligent.

1. Asset allocation and diversification by age and goal
2. Retirement accounts: 401(k) match, Roth vs traditional IRA
3. Reading a stock quote: market cap, P/E, dividend yield, 52-week range
4. Introduction to financial statements: income statement, balance sheet, cash flow
5. Where to find filings: SEC EDGAR, the 10-K (annual), 10-Q (quarterly), S-1 (IPO)
6. Dollar-cost averaging and staying calm in a downturn
7. Fees that quietly eat returns: expense ratios, advisory fees
8. Taxes 101: capital gains (short vs long term), tax-advantaged accounts
9. The IPO playbook: lock-ups, first-day pops, why waiting can be wise

Level up: written investment policy (goal, timeline, allocation), automatic monthly contribution set up, one 10-K summarized.

### Tier 3 — Steward (Advanced)

Goal: evaluate individual companies and manage a real portfolio. Theme: Matthew 25:21, managing much.

1. Deep 10-K/S-1 reading: risk factors, MD&A, footnotes, share structure
2. Valuation basics: P/E, price-to-sales, free cash flow, discounted cash flow intuition

4. Competitive analysis: comparing Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, and others
5. Sector cycles and bubbles: dot-com 2000 vs today's AI boom
6. Position sizing and rebalancing; never let one stock dominate
7. Real estate and REITs as diversification
8. Faith-based and values-aligned investing (screens, biblically responsible funds)
9. Working with professionals: advisors, CPAs, estate attorneys

Level up: a written company analysis (moat, financials, valuation, risks), a rebalanced portfolio, estate basics in place (will, beneficiaries).

### Tier 4 — Kingdom Builder (Expert)

4. Strategic giving: donor-advised funds, charitable remainder trusts, gifting appreciated stock

### What each tier learns from it

- **Seed:** what an IPO is; why the price on day one can jump or fall; why you never invest money you need soon.
- **Sower:** how to find and skim the S-1 on SEC EDGAR; revenue growth vs losses; what a lock-up is.

### How everyday investors can get shares

1. **At the IPO price (hard):** shares go mostly to institutions. Some brokers offer limited IPO access to eligible customers (confirm current programs at Fidelity, Schwab, E\*TRADE, Robinhood, SoFi, and Public).
2. **On day one (common):** buy ANT once it starts trading. Use a **limit order** so you do not overpay in the first-minute frenzy.
3. **Later (often wise):** wait for the first earnings report or the lock-up expiry (usually about 180 days), when prices can settle.
4. **Indirectly:** ETFs and funds that hold Anthropic, or companies with stakes in it (Google and Amazon have invested).

### The Apple comparison, told honestly

Apple went public in December 1980, and early holders who kept their shares for decades did extraordinarily well. But Apple also lost most of its value in the 1990s before its comeback, and for every Apple there are IPOs that never recovered their offer price. The lesson for the women: great companies can still be bad buys at the wrong price, and patience plus diversification is what made the Apple story possible.

## Interactive calculators spec

Six calculators make the app sticky. Every result shows a chart, three scenarios (conservative, moderate, aggressive), and the short disclaimer (see Disclaimers → Short version).

**Default return assumptions (editable):** Conservative 4% a year, Moderate 7%, Aggressive 10%; inflation 3%. Show an "after inflation" toggle.

### 1. Compound growth

Inputs: starting amount, monthly contribution, years, risk level (or custom %). Output: future value, total contributed vs growth (stacked area chart), year-by-year table. Example: $500 a month for 5 years at 7% grows to about $35,800 ($30,000 contributed).

### 2. Goal planner ("I want $1 million")

Inputs: target amount, years, starting amount, risk level. Output: required monthly contribution for each scenario, side by side. Example: $1M in 10 years from $0 at 7% needs about $5,800 a month; at 20 years, about $1,920. The lesson: time is the most powerful lever.

### 3. Salary-based investing plan

Inputs: annual salary, monthly take-home, monthly expenses, debts, employer 401(k) match %. Output: suggested savings range (10–20% of gross as a guideline), "capture the full match first" alert, and the order of operations: emergency fund, match, high-interest debt, Roth IRA, more retirement, taxable investing.

### 4. IPO and share calculator

Inputs: share price, number of shares (or dollar amount), holding years, scenario rates. Output: cost, value range in 1, 3, 5, and 10 years, and what percentage of her total portfolio the position would be (with a warning above 5–10%).

### 5. Risk profile quiz and allocation

Ten questions: age, time horizon, emergency fund status, income stability, dependents, reaction to a 30% drop, experience, goals. Output: risk band (Preserve, Balanced, Growth, Aggressive Growth) with a sample allocation pie (for example, Balanced = 60% stocks, 35% bonds, 5% cash) as education, not instruction.

### 6. Fee impact calculator

Inputs: amount, years, return, fee % (for example 0.05% index fund vs 1% advisor). Output: dollars lost to fees over time. Example: $100,000 for 30 years at 7%: a 1% fee costs roughly $190,000 compared with no fee.

Also useful: a debt payoff planner (avalanche vs snowball) and an emergency-fund tracker for Seed tier.

### Scenario 1 — Leaving an abusive relationship

For a woman in an unsafe marriage with no money of her own, the first goal is safety and a private cash cushion, not the stock market. Investments can drop just when she needs to leave; savings she can reach in a day cannot.

1. **Safety first.** Connect with the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788) for a safety plan, including a financial safety plan.
2. **Private access.** Use a safe device and email he cannot see. Consider a bank account at a different bank, with paperless statements to her private email.
3. **Build a "freedom fund."** Even $10 or $25 at a time in a high-yield savings account. Goal: first and last month's rent, a deposit, and a few weeks of living costs.
4. **Gather documents** (copies kept somewhere safe): IDs, birth certificates, Social Security cards, tax returns, account statements, titles, insurance policies.
5. **Build her own credit.** A secured card in her name only; check her credit report for debts opened in her name.
6. **Income path.** Training, certifications, side income, or job placement support.
7. **Investing comes after she is out and stable**, starting with an emergency fund and a Roth IRA.

App lesson link: "Financial abuse: recognizing it" (controlling all money, hiding accounts, sabotaging work, running up debt in her name).

**App safety design (important):** an option for discreet notifications (texts and emails that never mention money, investing, or abuse); no saved login on shared devices; and a way to pause all messages instantly. Texts to someone in an unsafe home can put her at risk.

### Scenario 2 — The widow whose husband was the breadwinner

Priority: stability before strategy. Pause big decisions for 6–12 months where possible.

1. Claim what is owed: life insurance, Social Security survivor benefits, pension or 401(k) beneficiary rollovers.
2. Keep 6–12 months of expenses in high-yield savings or Treasury bills.
3. Beware of anyone pressuring her to invest quickly, including people at church or family.
4. Then a conservative, income-focused mix; consider a fee-only fiduciary advisor.

### Scenario 3 — Starting from nothing

$25 a week is $1,300 a year. Start with a high-yield savings account, then fractional shares of a broad index fund. Show her the compound calculator: $100 a month at 7% for 30 years grows to about $122,000.

### Scenario 4 — Single mom with young children

Term life insurance and a will with a named guardian first; then the employer match, an emergency fund, and a small 529 contribution (grandparents can give to it too).

### Scenario 5 — High earner with a large portfolio

She needs diversification and risk control more than hot tips: no single stock above 5–10% of the portfolio, rebalance yearly, tax-aware placement, and a written plan for windfalls such as IPO gains.

### Hot sectors lesson (Steward and Kingdom Builder)

Themes drawing capital in 2026: AI models and software (Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft, Google), AI chips and infrastructure (NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom, TSMC), data centers and the power grid, cybersecurity, and healthcare innovation. Teach how to own a theme safely: a sector ETF instead of one stock, and a cap on how much of the portfolio sits in one theme. Pair it with the bubble lesson: in 2000, the internet was real, but many internet stocks fell 80% or more. A sector can be both transformative and overpriced.

## Advisors vs self-directed and fees

Typical advisory fees are about 1% of assets a year, not 10–20%. The 20% figure applies to hedge funds and private equity, which commonly charge about 2% a year plus 20% of profits. Even 1% compounds into a large sum, which is why this lesson matters.

| Path | How they get paid | Typical cost | Fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self-directed (Fidelity, Schwab, etc.) | Fund expense ratios; payment for order flow at some apps | $0 trades; index funds about 0.03–0.20% a year | Learners willing to do the work |
| Robo-advisor (Betterment, Wealthfront, Schwab Intelligent) | Small management fee | About 0.25% a year (some $0) | Hands-off beginners |
| Fee-only fiduciary advisor | Flat fee, hourly, or % of assets; no commissions | 0.5–1% a year or $1,500–$5,000 a plan | Complex situations, widows, high earners |
| Wirehouse advisor (Merrill Lynch, Morgan Stanley, UBS) | % of assets, plus commissions and product fees | About 1–1.5% a year, plus fund fees | Wealthy clients wanting full service |
| Commission-based broker or insurance agent | Commissions on products sold | Varies; can be 3–7% on some annuities and funds | Use with caution |
| Hedge funds and private equity | Management plus performance fee | About 2% plus 20% of profits | Accredited investors only |

**Questions every woman should ask an advisor:**

1. Are you a fiduciary at all times, in writing?
2. How exactly are you paid, and what will I pay in total each year in dollars?
3. Do you earn commissions or bonuses on products you recommend?
4. What is your investment approach, and how do you measure success?
5. Who holds my money (custodian), and can I see the accounts myself?

Check any advisor on FINRA BrokerCheck or the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site before signing.

**What Merrill Lynch and Schwab do well, which the app can teach:** goal-based planning, regular rebalancing, tax-loss harvesting, estate coordination, and behavioral coaching that keeps clients from panic-selling. A self-directed woman can copy most of this with a written plan and an annual review.

## Audience focus: women of color

Small print is acceptable only if it stays legible and next to the link; tiny, faded, or hidden text can fail the standard. **Decided format (November 2, 2026):** each partner card carries a visible label, **"Paid partner link*"**, right beside the button, and one footnote in small print at the bottom of the page: "* Dr. Cherie Banks may receive compensation if you open an account through a paid partner link. This does not affect our educational content or how partners are described." The word "paid" in the label matters: the FTC has said a label such as "paid link" can work when readers see it with the link, while "partner" alone may not tell consumers money changes hands. Confirm the final wording with the attorney.

## Your Credit Comeback: dispute rights and timelines

Verified November 2, 2026 against the Fair Credit Reporting Act (15 U.S.C. § 1681i), the CFPB, the FTC, and the National Consumer Law Center. Shown as a timeline in Your Credit Comeback:

- Day 0: the bureau receives the dispute (send certified mail, return receipt requested).
- Within 5 business days: the bureau must notify the company that reported the item.
- Within 30 days: the bureau must finish its investigation. Up to 45 days if the dispute followed the free annual report, or if the consumer sends relevant information during the 30 days (up to 15 more days).
- Within 5 business days after finishing: written results, plus a free updated report if anything changed.
- Inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable items must be promptly deleted or corrected. If the furnisher does not respond, the bureau must delete the item (NCLC). The statute does not literally say "missed deadline = automatic deletion," so the app frames it as "cannot be verified, must come off."
- If they miss the deadline: the new **missed deadline follow-up letter** (cites 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(1) and asks for deletion), a CFPB complaint at consumerfinance.gov/complaint, the state attorney general, and a consumer protection attorney (fee-shifting).
- Honest guardrails: deleted items can be reinserted if the furnisher certifies accuracy (with written notice within 5 business days); repeat disputes without new information can be rejected as frivolous; dispute only what you believe is inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable; accurate negative items generally stay 7 years (bankruptcy 10); disputing is free.
- Attorney flag: because the app supplies dispute letters, keep the Credit Repair Organizations Act review on the attorney list, and keep the app's letters truthful (CROA prohibits advising consumers to make untrue or misleading statements).
